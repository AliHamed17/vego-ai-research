## 6.1 Planning principles

### 6.1.1 Blocks, not months

The plan is expressed in **approximately three-month blocks** across a three-year horizon. It is deliberately not expressed month by month, and this is a methodological commitment rather than a drafting convenience.

Three of the four things that gate this research have latency that the candidate does not control:

- **independent expert returns** — the evidence base for every quantitative claim depends on named human reviewers completing blinded labelling and adjudication on their own schedule;
- **institutional decisions** — supervisor sign-off on question wording and study protocols, university process confirmation, and (under Plan A) ethics, data-custodian, and environment approvals;
- **external review** — paper submission-to-decision cycles.

A month-level schedule would assign precise dates to activities whose start conditions are held by other people. That produces a plan that is wrong within weeks and, worse, a plan that invites the reader to treat unverified dates as commitments. A block-level schedule states what must be *true* by the end of a roughly three-month window, names the gate that must clear first, and leaves the internal ordering to the weekly supervision cadence (§6.5.1), which is the correct instrument for week-level control.

The trade is explicit: the plan gives up apparent precision in exchange for being honest about which of its dates are load-bearing. Only the gates in §6.3 are load-bearing.

### 6.1.2 Why semester alignment

The blocks align to the academic calendar rather than to calendar quarters, for four reasons that bear directly on feasibility:

1. **The evidence source is course-generated.** The motivating corpus is student-produced domain models from taught courses. Any extension of the corpus, any second course setting under Plan B, and any new cohort of models becomes available on a teaching-semester rhythm, not a calendar-quarter rhythm.
2. **Expert reviewers are academics.** Independent labelling, adjudication, and second-panel recruitment compete with teaching load. Blocks that straddle a teaching semester and its examination period will systematically under-deliver on human-dependent tasks; blocks that place labelling campaigns in inter-semester windows will not.
3. **Supervision intensity varies by term.** Both supervisors carry teaching and examination duties. The weekly cadence continues year-round, but the depth of review realistically available differs between a teaching block and an inter-semester block, and design-freeze decisions are scheduled into the latter.
4. **Institutional milestones are semester-indexed.** Progress reporting, candidacy scheduling, and committee availability follow the academic year. Aligning the plan to it means that when A-14 is answered, the answer maps onto an existing block boundary instead of forcing a re-plan.

The precise term dates at the University of Haifa and Ben-Gurion University have **not** been verified for the years covered here. The block grid is therefore stated at quarter resolution — a two- or three-week shift in term boundaries does not move any block.

### 6.2.15 What is deliberately absent from this plan

No block's exit criterion requires a positive result. No block requires accuracy to improve, effort to fall, or reuse to generalise. Several exit criteria are written in the form "measured, **or** a recorded and reasoned statement of why it is not measurable", and both branches satisfy the plan. This is not defensive drafting: it follows from the fact that whether governed, reused expert judgment improves agentic assessment is an *empirical outcome* of the research, and a plan whose milestones depend on the answer coming out one way would create pressure to produce that answer.

### 6.3.3 Two structural properties of this dependency graph

**No research question depends on a gate outside the candidate's and supervisors' reach.** G-MED is held by external institutions; it gates only the optional Plan A extension. G-UNI is held by the university; it gates only dates, not content. Everything that determines whether the doctorate is answerable — G-RQ, G-LIT, G-LBL, G-PROTO, G-2ND — is held by the candidate and the supervision team.

**The expert-label gate is the critical path.** G-LBL sits upstream of every quantitative statement in the thesis. It is currently at zero. It is also the gate with the shortest path to clearing: it needs two named reviewers, a signed approval pack, and adjudication — no institution, no ethics board, no partner. This is why Block 0 and Y1 B1 both carry labelling mobilisation as a named deliverable, and why Y1 B2's exit criterion explicitly permits a recorded failure rather than pretending the gate will clear.

The gates are **sequential and cumulative**: use case → people → authorisation → ethics/privacy → environment → protocol. A gate passes only when every required item is verified, current, project-specific, and approved by every named approver; partial evidence produces no partial pass. If any of the use case, people, data source, authorisation, determination, environment, or protocol changes materially — or an approval expires — that gate and every later control return to blocked.

Because each addition sits *behind* a gate held by an external institution, none of them can be dated. In the vocabulary of §6.1.3 they are **Contingent**, and the block positions above are provisional placements, not schedule commitments.

### 6.5.1 Weekly supervision cadence

The weekly meeting is the plan's week-level control instrument, which is what allows the written plan to remain at block resolution. Each meeting follows a fixed shape: a short pre-read circulated in advance; completed artifacts shown with evidence links; blockers and required decisions stated explicitly; every decision recorded as approved, corrected, deferred, or rejected; one primary next task assigned with an owner and an acceptance check; and the proposal, action register, risk register, and literature workbook updated afterwards.

The cadence verifies logistics only. It approves no research decision by itself; approval exists only where a decision is logged.

### 6.5.2 Block-boundary reviews

**Status: Structural.** Four per year, at each block boundary, twelve across the plan. Each is a longer session with a fixed agenda:

1. Test the closing block's exit criteria one by one and record pass, partial, or fail with evidence.
2. Re-test each open gate in §6.3.1 and log its state.
3. Confirm or re-baseline the next block's deliverables.
4. Re-review the claim boundary: is any claim now being made that its evidence does not support, and is any claim now supportable that is still being withheld?
5. Record every change as a decision with old wording, new wording, rationale, approver, and effective date.

The fourth item runs in both directions deliberately. Over-claiming is the more serious failure, but under-claiming — leaving evidence unreported because a boundary was set conservatively at an earlier stage and never revisited — is also a failure, and a block-boundary review is the right place to catch it.

### 6.6.1 The two risks that actually govern this schedule

Of the ten, two dominate.

**R1 (expert labels)** is the critical path, for the reason given in §6.3.3: it sits upstream of every quantitative claim, it is currently at zero, and yet it is entirely within the supervision team's reach. Its mitigation is not a process control but a decision — name the reviewers and release the approval pack. Everything else in the mitigation column is secondary to that.

**R10 (single-person dependency)** is the risk the plan structurally cannot mitigate away, because a doctorate is by definition the work of one person. The block structure is the mitigation: a three-month window absorbs a two-week absence without any re-planning at all, whereas a month-level plan would require re-baselining. This is the last and most practical argument for the granularity that E14 requires — a plan expressed in blocks is a plan that survives contact with a real year.
